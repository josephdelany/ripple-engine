# The 1970s — what the literature says drove the episodes, and where our coding disagrees

*Session E, step E-5, 2026-09-02. Written against the corpus as it stands (the baseline is
`data/spine/AUDIT.md`) and against scholarship that was **actually opened for this pass**.
Sources not opened are named as not opened; nothing here rests on them. This essay does not
change any record: where it disagrees with a class or a date it says so, and the change is
proposed in the event's dossier for Joe.*

## What the corpus holds for this decade

Eight events, 1973-10-06 to 1979-11-04. At the baseline audit every one of the eight carried
drafting scaffolding, none had two independent source domains, none had a severity or
surprise code, and the median description was 85 characters. Three cited the bare root
`https://www.eia.gov`; five cited the NBER working paper below. The decade that gives the
engine its founding precedents was, in evidentiary terms, its thinnest.

## The episodes

### October 1973: war, then embargo

Hamilton dates the two events separately and precisely: "Syria and Egypt led an attack on
Israel that began on October 6, 1973. On October 17, the Arab members of the Organization of
Petroleum Exporting Countries announced an embargo on oil exports to selected countries
viewed as supporting Israel, which was followed by significant cutbacks in OPEC's total oil
production." Our two records carry exactly those dates, which is the one thing about this
decade's coding that the literature straightforwardly confirms.

He also gives the magnitude our records lack entirely: "Production from Arab members of OPEC
in November was down 4.4 mb/d from what it had been in September, a shortfall corresponding
to 7.5% of global output", and "Increases in production from other countries such as Iran
offset only a small part of this."

**Where our coding is challenged.** We code the embargo as `sanctions` with
`country.saudi_arabia:actor` and `country.usa:target`. Two problems follow from Hamilton's
own sentence. First, the actor was collective — the Arab members of OPEC acting as OAPEC —
and Saudi Arabia alone misstates it. Second, and more substantively, Hamilton reports a live
scholarly dispute about what kind of event this was: "Barsky and Kilian (2001) have emphasized
the importance of the economic motivations noted earlier rather than the Arab-Israeli War
itself as explanations for the embargo. They noted that the Arab oil producers had discussed
the possibility of an embargo prior to the war, and that the embargo was lifted without the
achievement of its purported political objectives." Hamilton does not concede the point —
"While there is no doubt that economic considerations were very important, Hamilton (2003,
p. 389) argued that geopolitical factors played a role as well" — but the dispute is real and
our closed class set forces a choice it cannot express. On the Barsky–Kilian reading, as
Hamilton reports it, the embargo has a large `opec_decision` component: an announced export
restriction accompanied by production cutbacks. We code it purely as `sanctions`.

This matters beyond a label. The engine retrieves analogs by class. If the founding event of
the modern oil-shock record is filed as a sanctions episode when the literature's revisionist
reading calls it a production decision, every later sanctions read inherits a precedent whose
mechanism is contested. The recommendation in the dossier is not to switch the class silently
but to record the contest on the record and let Joe rule.

### 1978–79: the Iranian revolution

Hamilton again supplies both the sequence and the magnitude our stubs lack: strikes spread to
the oil sector in the autumn of 1978, "bringing Iranian oil production down by 4.8 mb/d (or
7% of world production at the time) between October 1978 and January 1979. In January the
Shah fled the country, and Sheikh Khomeini seized power in February. About a third of the
lost Iranian production was made up by increases from Saudi Arabia and elsewhere."

Our three records — the oil-workers' strike (1978-10-31), the Shah's departure (1979-01-16)
and the revolution's culmination (1979-02-11) — sit correctly inside that sequence.

**Where our coding is challenged.** We class the oil-workers' strike as
`infrastructure_attack`. The codebook defines that class as a "Direct strike on production,
refining, or export infrastructure". A labour strike is not a strike in that sense; the word
is doing two different jobs. The physical effect was larger than most attacks in the corpus —
7% of world production — but the mechanism is not an attack, and the class as written does not
cover it. The same question arises for the 1977 Abqaiq fire, which the corpus also files as
`infrastructure_attack` and which, if accidental, has no actor at all; our record nonetheless
lists Saudi Arabia as both actor and target.

### The decade as a whole: what kind of shocks these were

The strand of the literature that matters most for this engine is the one that refuses to
treat "an oil shock" as a single kind of event. Ryan and Michieka summarise the position they
replicate: "The price of oil can rise because of a disruption to supply or an increase in
demand. The nature of the price change determines the dynamic effects." Their replication
using later data "provide[s] more evidence for Kilian's (2009) conclusions."

The BIS paper pushes the same point toward expectations, which is where it touches our
`surprise` variable directly: it finds that "identified future demand and supply shocks
explain about 30-35 percent of historical oil price fluctuations" and that "future oil supply
shocks are more than twice as important as realized and future demand shocks in accounting
for oil price developments."

That is an argument for the codebook's insistence that surprise be coded from what was
publicly expected the day before, and never from the size of the move. It is also a caution
about this decade specifically: every 1970s record currently has `surprise` NULL, so the
variable the literature says carries most of the price action is, for the founding decade,
entirely unmeasured. The dossiers propose codes only where a retrieved contemporaneous source
establishes what was expected; where none does, they leave the null and say why. A null here
is a measurement, not an omission.

## What this decade cannot yet settle

Hamilton's chronology is a secondary source and cannot establish, on its own, what a market
participant knew on a given morning. That is what the primary record is for, and it is why
the dossiers for this decade go to the Foreign Relations of the United States series and to
the presidential documents rather than resting on the chronology that the seed file already
cited. Where a primary document could not be retrieved, the dossier says `partial` and names
the clause it fails.
