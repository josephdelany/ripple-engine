# The 1980s — the decade the archive will not open, and the decade our classes fit worst

*Session E, step E-5, 2026-09-02. Written against the corpus as it stands and against
scholarship actually opened for this pass. Sources not opened are named as not opened.
This essay changes no record: disagreements are recorded here and proposed in the event's
dossier, for Joe.*

## What the corpus holds for this decade

Eleven events, 1980-01-23 to 1988-08-20. Ten of the eleven carried drafting scaffolding at
the baseline audit; six cited the bare root `https://www.eia.gov`; nine had no severity and
no surprise code. Two — the Bridgeton mining and Operation Praying Mantis — already cited
real documents and carried codes, and they remain the two best records of the decade.

## The archival problem, stated first because it shapes everything else

The 1970s dossiers rest on primary documents because the *Foreign Relations of the United
States* volumes covering them are published. For the 1980s they are not. Checked, not
assumed: FRUS 1981–1988 **Volume XX (Iran)** and **Volume XXI (Iraq)** are both marked
"Being Cleared" and are unpublished, and FRUS 1969–1976 **Volume X** (Iran, January 1977 –
November 1979) is likewise unpublished. The declassification queue, not the researcher's
effort, is the binding constraint on this decade.

The consequence is recorded rather than disguised. Four of the six 1984–1986 records rest
on scholarly secondary sources — principally Cordesman and Wagner's CSIS volumes on the
Iran–Iraq war, and the *Middle East Report* — with no primary document behind them, and
their dossiers say `partial — fails (a)` for exactly that reason. `kharg_strikes_1985`
reaches only a single domain and is the weakest record in the tier. Where a primary
document *does* exist the dossiers use it: FRUS 1981–1988 Volume I yielded a document for
the 1987 reflagging, the American Presidency Project yielded Reagan's report to Congress on
Praying Mantis, and UN Peacekeeping's own mission history yielded the ceasefire dates.

This is worth saying plainly because it inverts the usual assumption. The engine's
historical arm is thin not only because the corpus was built recently, but because for the
Gulf in the 1980s the primary record is still classified.

## The scholarship opened for this pass

Hamilton's *Historical Oil Shocks* (NBER WP 16790, retrieved and read in full) is again the
anchor, and again a working paper whose own cover says it is not peer-reviewed. On the
central episode of the decade it is unambiguous: "Saudi Arabia voluntarily shut down 3/4 of
its production between 1981 and 1985, though this was not enough to prevent a 25% decline
in the nominal price of oil and significantly bigger decline in the real price. The Saudis
abandoned those efforts, beginning to ramp production back up in 1986, causing the price of
oil to collapse from $27/barrel in 1985 to $12/barrel at the low point in 1986."

He adds the point our corpus structurally cannot represent: "Although a favorable
development from the perspective of oil consumers, this represented an 'oil shock' for the
producers." Our codebook codes severity as "expected disruption" on a 1–5 scale built for
supply loss. A price collapse is a shock with the sign reversed, and the scale has no way to
say so.

**Not opened, and not relied on:** Mabro on the 1986 collapse, Skeet's *OPEC*, Parra's *Oil
Politics*, Yergin, Gause on Gulf security. These are the standard works for this decade and
their absence is the main scholarly weakness of this essay.

## Where our coding is challenged, episode by episode

**The 1986 collapse is dated to New Year's Day and cannot be.** `opec_price_collapse_1986`
carries `1986-01-01` at day precision. The loader that inserted it stamps `day` on every
deep-history event regardless of evidence (reported to Session A separately), and here the
false precision is flagrant. Worse, the dossier found **two** defensible anchors and could
not adjudicate between them from retrieved sources: Yamani's September 1985 remarks at the
Oxford Energy Seminar that Saudi Arabia was "being forced to abandon its official price
policy and essentially join the netback bandwagon", and OPEC's 71st Conference in Vienna on
10–11 December 1985. The Conference communiqué itself could not be retrieved — `opec.org`
returns HTTP 402 — so the choice is put to Joe rather than manufactured. Either anchor puts
the decision in **1985**, not 1986, which means the event is currently dated to the year of
the consequence rather than the year of the decision.

**One event names the wrong island.** `iraq_kharg_1986` is titled and identified for Kharg,
but the database's own title text and every retrieved source describe the 12 August 1986
raid on **Sirri** Island — the terminal Iran had built precisely because it believed Sirri
lay beyond Iraqi reach. The event_id will have to keep its name or be migrated with care,
since the engine keys on it; the dossier records the mismatch rather than silently
retitling.

**A ceasefire is dated to the day it took effect, not the day it was knowable.** The UN's
own mission history states that on 8 August 1988 the Secretary-General "announced the
agreement of both Iran and Iraq to a ceasefire with effect from 0300 GMT on 20 August", and
that "The ceasefire came into effect at 0300 GMT on 20 August 1988." Our record uses
1988-08-20. The codebook's date rule is explicit that `event_date` is "the first day the
market could have known", which is 8 August. This is the clearest date error in the tier and
the dossier proposes the correction.

**Three classes do not fit, and the closed set offers nowhere to put them.** The Carter
Doctrine is filed as `policy_response`, a class the codebook defines as deliberate
government market intervention with strategic-reserve releases as its example; a declaratory
security commitment is not that, and its `country.iran:target` coding is unsupported — the
"outside force" was the Soviet Union. The Iran–Iraq ceasefire is filed the same way, and a
de-escalation is not a market intervention either. The Tanker War is filed as a single
`chokepoint_disruption` on 27 March 1984; the dossier establishes that this was a real,
datable act — Iraq's first Exocet strike from Super Étendard aircraft, hitting two small
tankers southwest of Kharg — but the record's *title* describes a multi-year campaign, and
the source itself dates the sustained escalation to April onward.

The pattern across all three is the same: our seven classes describe **things that break
supply**, and the decade's most consequential events were a producer decision to stop
defending price, a security guarantee, and a war ending. A closed set is the right design —
it is what stops post-hoc reclassification — but this decade shows where it is blind, and
the blindness is worth stating in the paper rather than discovered by a reviewer.

**One event may not belong in the corpus at all.** `iran_air_655_1988` is well sourced,
with Reagan's own statement and the ICJ case record. But the codebook's inclusion criterion
3 requires a mechanism by which the event could affect oil supply, demand or the risk
premium, statable in one sentence. The dossier states plainly that no infrastructure,
tanker or chokepoint was involved and that the channel is a speculative risk premium with no
evidenced market effect. It is included here as a record that meets the *sourcing* standard
while failing the *inclusion* standard — the opposite of the 1977 Abqaiq fire, which may
describe a real supply event but has no retrievable source at all.

## What this decade tells the engine

Two things, both uncomfortable. First, the analog pool for any modern Gulf read is built on
records whose primary documentation is still classified, so their coding rests on
historians' reconstruction rather than on contemporaneous documents — and the walk's
filtration cannot distinguish the two. Second, the decade's largest price event was a
producer decision that our severity scale cannot express and our date field misplaces by a
year. Neither is a reason to drop the decade. Both are reasons to carry the caveat in the
paper's data section, where the thinness of the historical arm is already conceded.
