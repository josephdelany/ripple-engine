# The 2020s — half the corpus, and the decade where policy became the shock

*Session E, step E-5, 2026-09-02. Written against the corpus as it stands and against
scholarship actually opened for this pass. Sources not opened are named as not opened. This
essay changes no record.*

## What the corpus holds

One hundred and fifty events to 2026-06-17, **just under half the entire corpus**: thirty-six
policy responses, twenty-eight infrastructure attacks, twenty-six sanctions, twenty-three
conflict escalations, fifteen OPEC decisions, thirteen chokepoint disruptions, nine demand
shocks. Ten still carry drafting scaffolding and eleven cite Wikipedia as their only source.
The median description is 149 characters. Eighty-seven carry an IES-90 level and sixty are
uncovered.

`policy_response` is now the **largest** class, which no earlier decade shows. In the 1970s
the state acted by embargo; in the 2020s it acts by reserve release, price cap and licence.

## The scholarship opened for this pass

Baumeister, *Pandemic, War, Inflation: Oil Markets at a Crossroads?*, NBER Working Paper
31496, July 2023, 36 pp., retrieved and read. It was "written for the panel discussion on
'Structural Change in Energy Markets and Implications for Inflation' at the ECB Forum on
Central Banking, Sintra, June 26-28, 2023", and like every NBER working paper its cover
states it has not been peer-reviewed. The BIS paper (Fueki et al., 2018, read) supplies the
expectations result quoted in the 2010s essay.

**Not opened, and not relied on:** any book-length account of the pandemic or the Ukraine
war's energy dimension, and the IEA and IMF publications on the 2020 collapse, which were
sought and not retrieved (`imf.org` returned 403).

## What the literature says the decade was about

Baumeister's abstract states the frame: "The COVID-19 pandemic as well as the Russian
invasion of Ukraine have had profound effects on the global energy landscape, with some of
the longer-lasting effects still unfolding." Her account of 2020 is precise about magnitude
and about who did what: "At the onset of the pandemic, when oil demand collapsed as a result
of worldwide lockdowns, US production plummeted by almost three million barrels per day
(mbpd) within two months. The steep fall in global oil demand also called for production cuts
from OPEC+, but Russia initially refused to cooperate with OPEC to stabilize oil prices, which
triggered a price war between the two leading oil exporters, Russia and Saudi Arabia."

On the policy side she describes instruments that did not exist in earlier decades: "the price
cap policy was introduced to supplement the EU embargo on seaborne Russian oil exports, and
record volumes of oil were released from government-controlled emergency stockpiles." On the
reserves specifically: "While not a new policy instrument, the scale and duration of drawdowns
from the emergency reserves" is what changed. And on shale, the elasticity the 2010s essay
described is now impaired: "in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, both physical
constraints and corporate demands have dim[inished]" the ability to ramp quickly.

## Where our coding is challenged

**A demand collapse and a price war are one episode, and we have no way to say so.** In
Baumeister's telling, the 2020 collapse is a demand shock, a failed OPEC+ negotiation and a
deliberate Saudi–Russian production war, interacting. Our closed set forces these into
separate rows of different classes with no relation between them. The engine then retrieves
them as independent analogs. This is the sharpest instance of a limitation the 1980s essay
also found: the classes describe events, and the decade's defining episodes are *interactions*.

**`policy_response` is now the largest class and its definition is the one under challenge.**
The 2026-07-23 codebook amendment defines it as "deliberate government/agency market
interventions (e.g. coordinated SPR/IEA strategic-reserve releases)". For a reserve release
that is exact. But Session E's pre-1990 pass found the same class holding a declaratory
security doctrine and a ceasefire, and the proposed codebook amendment
(`docs/spine/CODEBOOK_AMENDMENT_PROPOSED.md`) records the gap. With thirty-six records in this
decade alone, the class's boundaries matter more than they did when it held two.

**The price cap has no class at all.** It is neither a sanction on a producer in the
codebook's one-directional sense, nor a production decision, nor a reserve release. It is a
consumer-side cartel setting the terms of a producer's sales. Baumeister's judgement is that
if it works, "this new policy instrument has the potential to change the balance of power
between producers and consumers in energy markets with implications lasting long after the
war in Ukraine ends." A corpus that cannot name it will code it as `sanctions` and lose what
is distinctive about it.

**Selection is at its worst here.** Half the corpus falls in the most recent six years, which
is where the compilers' attention and the available sources both concentrate. The paper's data
section already concedes the distribution is "heavily recent". The measured consequence is that
any analog pool for a modern read is dominated by events from the same few years, the same few
programmes and, in eleven cases, the same encyclopaedia.

## What this decade tells the engine

That the corpus is best understood as a dense recent record with a historical tail, and that
its recent density is concentrated in policy actions whose class definition is the one the
pre-1990 pass found least well specified. The two ends of the corpus fail differently: the
1970s and 1980s for want of documents, the 2020s for want of a vocabulary that can hold a
price cap, a price war and a pandemic in the same frame.
