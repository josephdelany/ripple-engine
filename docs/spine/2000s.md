# The 2000s — the decade whose biggest price move had no event in it

*Session E, step E-5, 2026-09-02. Written against the corpus as it stands and against
scholarship actually opened for this pass. Sources not opened are named as not opened. This
essay changes no record.*

## What the corpus holds

Forty-three events, 2000-03-28 to 2009-08-06, and the composition is lopsided: **eighteen of
the forty-three are OPEC decisions**, against seven infrastructure attacks, six conflict
escalations, six policy responses, three sanctions, two demand shocks and one chokepoint
disruption. Eleven cite Wikipedia as their only source, more than any other decade. The
median description is 150 characters. Twenty-six of the forty-three are IES-90 uncovered,
which is expected: OPEC and policy records have no ICB, MID or UCDP counterpart.

## The scholarship opened for this pass

Hamilton's *Historical Oil Shocks* (NBER 16790, read in full) covers this decade in detail
and is the source of every quotation below. The BIS working paper on expectations (Fueki et
al., BIS 725, 2018, read) bears on the class question at the end. **Not opened:** Yergin,
Parra, Kilian's articles, Simmons and Gately, who are known here only as Hamilton cites them.

## The central finding, which is about an absence

The largest sustained oil price rise of the decade had no geopolitical event behind it, and
Hamilton says so directly: **"Unlike many other historical oil shocks, there was no dramatic
geopolitical event associated with this."** His account of 2007–08 is a story about
production that stopped growing while demand did not: "World oil consumption grew 5 mb/d over
this period, or 3% per year"; "Production from the North Sea accounted for 8% of world
production in 2001, but had fallen more than 2 mb/d from these levels by the end of 2007";
Mexico's Cantarell "saw its production decline 1 mb/d between 2005 and 2008"; and "Saudi
production was 850,000 barrels a day lower in 2007 than it had been in 2005."

Our corpus cannot represent any of that. Field maturity, decline rates and a residual
supplier declining to be residual are **states**, not dated events, and an event-keyed spine
has no row for them. What the corpus holds for 2007–08 instead is a sequence of OPEC meetings,
a pipeline explosion, a Turkish incursion and Lehman.

This is the same result the Big Moves census reaches from the opposite direction when it
finds that 35% of the largest Brent moves have no corpus event at all. The census measures
the gap; Hamilton names its largest instance. Both belong in the paper's data section, and
the honest statement is that an event corpus is the wrong instrument for a slow supply
plateau, not that the corpus is missing rows it could have had.

## A real omission, found by reading the literature

Hamilton: "A general strike eliminated 2.1 mb/d of oil production from Venezuela in December
of 2002 and January of 2003. This was followed shortly after by the U.S. attack on Iraq,
which removed an additional 2.2 mb/d over April to July. These would both be characterized as
exogenous geopolitical events, and they show up dramatically in Figure 11. **Kilian (2008)
argued they should be included in the list of postwar oil shocks.**"

Checked against the corpus: the Iraq invasion is present (`iraq_war_begins_2003`). **The
Venezuelan general strike is not.** It survives only inside the title of an OPEC decision,
`opec_hike_jan_2003`, "OPEC raises quota 6.5% amid Venezuela strike". A 2.1 mb/d supply loss
that a leading authority argues belongs on the list of postwar oil shocks is absent from a
corpus of oil-shock events, and is visible in ours only as background to somebody else's
meeting.

Hamilton himself is careful not to overstate the pair — "the affected supply was a much
smaller share of the global market than many of the other events discussed here, and the
disruptions had little apparent effect on global oil supplies" — which makes the omission
more interesting, not less: the corpus excludes an event the literature debates, and records
no trace of the debate.

## Where our coding is challenged

**The OPEC monoculture.** Eighteen of forty-three records are OPEC quota decisions, most of
them scheduled conferences whose outcomes were briefed in advance. The codebook's surprise
scale reserves 1 for exactly this — "Fully anticipated; scheduled and consensus-expected
(e.g. an OPEC meeting whose outcome was pre-briefed)" — yet these records mostly carry
surprise 2 or 3. If the BIS finding is right that "future oil supply shocks are more than
twice as important as realized and future demand shocks in accounting for oil price
developments", then how a scheduled meeting was priced beforehand is the whole question, and
coding it at 3 by default throws away the variable that matters.

**Announcement versus effect.** An OPEC communiqué and the date a quota change takes effect
are different days. The codebook dates an event to the first day the market could have known,
which is the communiqué. The dossiers were asked to establish both and propose the
announcement date where they differ.

**A recession is not an event.** `us_recession_2001_onset` is dated 2001-03-01, the NBER
peak. The NBER Business Cycle Dating Committee announced that peak in November 2001, eight
months later. Under the codebook's own date rule the knowable date and the peak date are not
the same day, and the dossier addresses it explicitly.

## What this decade tells the engine

That the corpus's coverage is not uniform in kind. The 1970s and 1980s records are supply
interruptions; the 2000s records are mostly meetings. An analog retrieved from this decade is
therefore likely to be a scheduled OPEC decision, and the engine's similarity over the state
vector will be doing most of the work, because the events themselves are nearly
interchangeable. That is worth knowing before reading any 2000s-heavy analog pool.
