# The 2010s — sanctions become the dominant instrument, and shale changes what a shock means

*Session E, step E-5, 2026-09-02. Written against the corpus as it stands and against
scholarship actually opened for this pass. Sources not opened are named as not opened. This
essay changes no record.*

## What the corpus holds

Eighty-five events, more than the previous four decades combined: twenty-one sanctions,
sixteen conflict escalations, fourteen OPEC decisions, thirteen policy responses, ten
chokepoint disruptions, eight infrastructure attacks, three demand shocks. Eleven still carry
drafting scaffolding and five cite Wikipedia as their only source. The median description is
148 characters, the corpus median exactly. Fifty-five of the eighty-five carry an IES-90
level, the best coverage of any decade, because the class mix is heavily geopolitical.

The composition is the story: **sanctions become the single largest class**, which no earlier
decade shows. The instrument of oil geopolitics moved from embargo and war to designation
and licence.

## The scholarship opened for this pass

The BIS working paper (Fueki, Higashi, Higashio, Nakajima, Ohyama and Tamanyu, *Identifying
oil price shocks and their consequences: the role of expectations in the crude oil market*,
BIS Working Papers No 725, May 2018, read) is the source that speaks to this decade directly.
Baumeister's *Pandemic, War, Inflation: Oil Markets at a Crossroads?* (NBER 31496, July 2023,
read) is written about the 2020s but its account of the shale sector's behaviour begins here.
Hamilton's chronology ends in 2010 and is not stretched past it.

**Not opened, and not relied on:** Yergin, Parra, Kilian's articles, and any dedicated
history of the shale revolution. This decade has the weakest scholarly footing of the six
essays and that is stated rather than disguised.

## What the literature says the decade was about

The BIS paper's finding is the one that bears hardest on our design: "identified future demand
and supply shocks explain about 30-35 percent of historical oil price fluctuations. In
particular, future oil supply shocks are more than twice as important as realized and future
demand shocks in accounting for oil price developments." It adds that "the financial factors
and the development of shale-oil technology are additional relevant sources of oil price
fluctuations."

Two consequences follow for a corpus built on dated events.

First, **expectations of future supply matter more than realised supply**. Our codebook
already knows this in its own vocabulary: the `surprise` scale exists precisely because "the
engine showed it matters", and it instructs coders to work from "what was publicly expected
the day before, using contemporaneous reporting — never with hindsight". The BIS result is
the academic form of the same claim. It also raises the cost of the corpus's current state,
where `surprise` is coded but its evidentiary basis is rarely recorded on the record.

Second, **shale is a supply elasticity, not an event**. Baumeister describes the mechanism:
"Historically, US shale oil producers have been able to ramp up production in a short time
span to take advantage of rising oil prices". A producer base that responds within months
changes what any given disruption means, and it does so continuously rather than on a date.
Like spare capacity in 1990 and field maturity in 2007, it belongs in the state vector, and
the corpus has no row for it.

## Where our coding is challenged

**Sanctions are a process, and the corpus dates them as points.** Twenty-one sanctions records
in one decade, most of them designations, waivers, licences and expiries in long-running
programmes against Iran, Russia and Venezuela. Each is genuinely dated, so the codebook's
date rule is satisfiable. But the analytical object — a sanctions regime tightening over
years — is not a sequence of independent shocks, and the walk's clustering rule (reads within
35 days are one cluster) is the only thing standing between the engine and treating them as
independent draws. Whether 35 days is the right window for a sanctions programme is a
question for the protocol, not for this essay, and it is flagged for Session B.

**The direction problem, again.** The 1970s essay recorded that the `sanctions` class is
defined as measures "on a producer", while in 1973 the producers were the actors. In this
decade the definition fits well, since the targets are producers. The class is therefore
doing different work in different decades under the same name, which is worth stating where
the corpus is described.

**The classes with no independent outcome.** Thirty of the eighty-five are IES-90 uncovered:
the OPEC, policy and demand records. That is by construction and is not a defect, but it means
the escalation target is scored on roughly two thirds of this decade, and the third it skips
is the third where the instrument was economic rather than military.

## What this decade tells the engine

That the corpus's centre of gravity has moved. A modern read retrieves mostly 2010s and 2020s
analogs, and those are mostly sanctions episodes inside long programmes. The engine's
similarity is therefore being asked to distinguish among events of one kind, in one period,
under one instrument. That is a narrower task than "read any oil shock in history", and the
walk's results should be read as evidence about that narrower task.
