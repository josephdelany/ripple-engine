# The 1990s — one enormous event, then a decade the corpus barely sees

*Session E, step E-5, 2026-09-02. Written against the corpus as it stands and against
scholarship actually opened for this pass. Sources not opened are named as not opened. This
essay changes no record; disagreements are proposed in each event's dossier, for Joe.*

## What the corpus holds

Sixteen events, 1990-08-02 to 1999-03-23: five sanctions, four OPEC decisions, three
conflict escalations, three demand shocks, one infrastructure attack. Six carried drafting
scaffolding at the baseline; **four cite Wikipedia as their only source**; the median
description is 175 characters, the longest of any decade and still a sentence. Nine of the
sixteen carry an IES-90 level and seven are uncovered, the uncovered ones being the OPEC and
macro records that have no ICB, MID or UCDP counterpart by construction.

## The scholarship opened for this pass

Hamilton's *Historical Oil Shocks* (NBER 16790, read in full) again, and for the Asian
crisis its numbers are unambiguous. **Not opened, and not relied on:** Yergin, Skeet, Parra,
Gause, and Kilian's own articles, which are reached here only through Hamilton's account and
through the arXiv replication's abstract.

## The episodes

### 1990–91: the one event the engine is built around

Hamilton: "By 1990, Iraqi production had returned to its levels of the late 1970s, only to
collapse again (and bring Kuwait's substantial production down with it) when the country
invaded Kuwait in August 1990. The two countries accounted for nearly 9% of world
production." He adds the part our corpus does not record: "The price spike proved to be of
short duration, however, as the Saudis used the substantial excess capacity that they had
been maintaining throughout the decade to restore world production by November to the levels
seen prior to the conflict."

That second sentence is the interesting one for this project. The invasion is coded severity
5 and surprise 5, and both look right. But the reason the spike faded was **spare capacity**,
a state variable, not an event. An event-keyed corpus records the shock and cannot record the
buffer that absorbed it. The engine's state vector is where that belongs, and the 1990 read
is the natural test of whether it is there.

A provenance note: `iraq_invades_kuwait_1990` cites `digitallibrary.un.org`, which returns
HTTP 403 to scripts today. The corpus's single most important record currently has a
citation a reader cannot open.

### 1997–98: the decade's second act is a demand shock, and we code three of them

Hamilton on the Asian crisis: "The dollar price of oil soon followed them down, falling below
$12 a barrel by the end of 1998. In real terms, that was the lowest price since 1972, and a
price that perhaps never will be seen again."

Our corpus codes the Thai baht float, the Korean IMF programme and the Russian default as
`demand_shock`. The class is defensible — the codebook defines it as "A macro/health/policy
event that abruptly changes demand" — but the codebook's inclusion criterion 3 also demands a
mechanism statable in one sentence, and for a currency float the chain to a barrel of oil is
several links long. The dossiers were asked to state that mechanism explicitly for each, or
to record that it is weak. This is the decade where the corpus stops being a record of
supply interruptions and starts being a record of macroeconomic events, and nothing in the
codebook marks the transition.

### The 1998–99 OPEC decisions and the sourcing floor

Four OPEC decisions close the decade, two of them sourced to Wikipedia's "1998 world oil
market chronology". `opec.org` returns HTTP 402 and cannot be used, so these records must be
re-sourced to an institutional or wire document reporting the decision and its date, or
marked partial. The general problem is stated in the 2000s essay, where it is much larger.

## Where our coding is challenged

The literature's main quarrel with a corpus like ours in this decade is one of **omission
rather than misclassification**. Hamilton treats 1990–91 and 1997–98 as the decade's two oil
shocks, and both are present. The gap is that the corpus has no record of the mechanism that
ended the 1990 spike, and no way to express that a price *fall* to a real level unseen since
1972 was itself the event of 1998, rather than a consequence of events we did record.

## What this decade tells the engine

The 1990s is where the corpus is thinnest relative to how much the market moved: sixteen
events for a decade containing the largest supply shock since 1979 and the deepest real price
low since 1972. Any analog read for a modern Gulf crisis draws on this decade, and it draws
on sixteen records of which four currently cite an encyclopaedia and one cites a page that
returns 403.
