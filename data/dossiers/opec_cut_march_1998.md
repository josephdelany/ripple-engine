## Narrative

Facing collapsing prices, Mexico brokered a secret Mexico-Venezuela meeting in Miami on 4 March 1998, which persuaded Venezuela — previously seen as implacably committed to output growth — to consider coordinated cuts [S1]. That opening led to a secret trilateral meeting of Saudi Arabia, Venezuela and Mexico in Riyadh on 21-22 March 1998, whose communiqué set a target of 1.6 to 2.0 million barrels a day in total cuts sought from all exporters, OPEC and non-OPEC combined [S1]. Oil prices rose $1.50-$2.20 a barrel on the Riyadh news [S1]. OPEC then met publicly in Vienna on 30 March 1998 to formalize a cuts policy; unlike the secret Riyadh meeting, which "impressed the market," the public Vienna meeting "depressed it" as traders judged the agreed cuts insufficient [S1]. Mabro identifies a structural flaw: the cuts' baseline was "actual production" in February 1998, established only by averaging estimates from reporting agencies, not by disclosed OPEC data [S1]. Independently, RFE/RL's 1999 report on OPEC's subsequent 23 March 1999 cut describes it as "the third [cutback agreement] in two years," after "both previous accords were sabotaged by overproduction by individual members" — corroborating, without dating or quantifying it further, that a first 1998 cut accord existed [S2]. This dossier could not confirm the specific 1.245 million b/d OPEC-only figure now in the database; sources retrieved this session establish only Riyadh's 1.6-2.0 mb/d target for all exporters combined. ## Not known at the time

Per [S1], OPEC's February 1998 production baseline (against which cuts were measured) was itself contested — established only by averaging secondary reporting-agency estimates, not by disclosed OPEC data — so market participants could not know with confidence how large the "real" cut was relative to actual output, only relative to a disputed estimate. It was also not knowable at the time whether Venezuela's secretly-negotiated change of position would hold once tested publicly against its own domestic investment commitments [S1]. [S1] separately notes OPEC's credibility problem from "repeated roll-overs" of past commitments — compliance, not just the decision itself, was an open question going into and coming out of Vienna.
