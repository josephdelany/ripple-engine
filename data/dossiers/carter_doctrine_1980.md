## Narrative

On the evening of 23 January 1980 President Carter told a joint session of Congress that "an attempt by any outside force to gain control of the Persian Gulf region will be regarded as an assault on the vital interests of the United States ... repelled by any means necessary, including military force" [S1][S2]. The declaration followed the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 and came amid the Iran hostage crisis. It was a policy statement, not a market intervention: no barrels were shut in, released or rerouted that day, and no producer or transit state was named as a target, the "outside force" language being aimed at the Soviet Union [S1]. A State Department historical note describes the address as the trigger for later steps to increase naval presence in the Indian Ocean and expand basing, including Diego Garcia, though no exact wording for those details was retrieved this session [S2]. The doctrine was not a bolt from the blue: nine days earlier the Christian Science Monitor reported Carter was "preparing a major speech to set out a 'new American strategic doctrine'", drew a parallel to the 1947 Truman Doctrine, and cautioned "it would be premature to say there's a grand design" [S3]. Known on the day: a specific US commitment to use force if necessary to keep the Gulf out of hostile control. Not claimed here: any Gulf oil-trade volume, since no retrieved source ties the speech to a barrel figure.

## Class

Proposed class: `policy_response`, as currently coded, but the fit is contested and the challenge is recorded here rather than silently resolved. Codebook clause for `policy_response` (amendment 2026-07-23): "deliberate government/agency **market** interventions (e.g. coordinated SPR/IEA strategic-reserve releases). Severity = scale of the intervention." The Carter Doctrine is a declaratory security commitment about a hypothetical future contingency (an outside power seizing the Gulf); it moved no barrels, released no reserves, and set no price or production policy on the day — S1 and S2 together support only a security-guarantee reading, not a market intervention. None of the other five pre-registered classes fit better: it is not `conflict_escalation` (no war, invasion, or military escalation occurred that day — S1's own text addresses a hypothetical), not `sanctions`, `chokepoint_disruption`, `infrastructure_attack`, or `demand_shock`. This dossier recommends Joe treat `policy_response` as the least-bad available label while noting the codebook's own definition does not cleanly cover a declaratory doctrine with no accompanying market action, and that a future codebook revision might want a distinct class (e.g. `security_guarantee`) for this kind of event.

## Not known at the time

On January 23, 1980, it was not known whether or how the doctrine would ever be invoked; no Soviet move against the Gulf materialized in the doctrine's own terms, and the practical U.S. buildup (Rapid Deployment Force, Diego Garcia expansion) described in S2 unfolded over subsequent years, not that day. The Christian Science Monitor's January 14 preview [S3] shows the *fact* of a forthcoming doctrine was anticipated, but not its exact wording — the "repelled by any means necessary, including military force" formulation is not attested in any source retrieved this session prior to January 23 itself.
