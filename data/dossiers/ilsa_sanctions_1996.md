## Narrative

President Clinton signed the Iran and Libya Sanctions Act (H.R. 3107, Public Law 104-172) on August 5, 1996, at 9:42 a.m. in the Oval Office [S3]. The law directs sanctions against any person making a qualifying investment — "$40,000,000 or more," or aggregated investments of at least $10 million each reaching that sum within 12 months — that directly and significantly contributes to Iran's or Libya's petroleum-resource development capability, with thresholds falling to $20 million and $5 million for non-cooperating jurisdictions [S1][S2][S4]. Clinton framed the bill as denying Iran and Libya "money they need to finance international terrorism" and limiting resources for weapons of mass destruction, and as pressure on Libya over the Pan Am 103 bombing suspects [S3]. The bill had passed the House June 18-19 and the Senate (amended) July 16, with the House concurring July 23 — roughly two weeks before signature [S2][S5]. The EU's Trade Commissioner immediately said the bloc would "act to defend its rights and interests" against the law [S5]. The statute targets future foreign investment in petroleum development, not currently flowing Iranian or Libyan exports [S1][S2].

## Class

Proposed class: `sanctions` (unchanged). Codebook clause: "`sanctions` | Sanctions imposed, tightened, or lifted on a producer." ILSA is a secondary/extraterritorial sanction — it does not sanction Iran or Libya directly, but sanctions third-country persons who invest in their petroleum sectors above the statutory threshold, with the express purpose of pressuring those two producer states [S1][S3][S4]. This is the same asymmetry already flagged in the `oapec_embargo_1973` dossier for the codebook's phrasing; the mechanism (using economic pressure to constrain a producer's oil-sector development) is unambiguously within the closed set's intent, and no other class (`opec_decision`, `conflict_escalation`, `infrastructure_attack`, `chokepoint_disruption`, `demand_shock`, `policy_response`) fits an act of Congress targeting third-party investment. ## Not known at the time

On August 5, 1996, it was not yet known how the President would exercise the Act's waiver and enforcement discretion — the statute requires a presidential *determination* before sanctions attach to any specific investor, and no source retrieved this session documents any company being sanctioned on or immediately after this date. The EU's threatened WTO challenge and retaliatory measures, flagged as likely "within the following month" by [S5], had not yet materialized. Whether the $40 million threshold would actually deter major foreign investment in Iranian or Libyan petroleum development — the practical test of the law's disruption potential — was not established by anything retrieved this session; the statute's severity is a *threat structure*, and its realized effect on physical barrels is a separate, unretrieved question.
