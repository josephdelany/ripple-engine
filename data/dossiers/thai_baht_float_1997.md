## Narrative

On 2 July 1997, "the Thai authorities abandoned the pegging of their currency," ending the baht's link to the dollar after "a first major assault on the baht in May and sustained downward pressures in the subsequent weeks" [S1]. The Bank of Thailand had spent down reserves defending the peg before floating [S1]. By September 30, 1997 the Federal Reserve's own policy record acknowledged "recent depreciations of Southeast Asian currencies" as a live concern for U.S. monetary policy [S2]; by November 12 the FOMC recorded that "turmoil in Asian financial markets and economies would tend to damp output and prices in the United States" [S2] — an explicit contemporaneous statement that the crisis was expected to weigh on demand generally, oil included by extension though not named specifically. The oil-market channel is scholarly, not contemporaneous: Hamilton's retrospective notes the newly industrialized Asian economies' "contribution to world petroleum consumption at the time was modest," but that "investors developed doubts about the Asian growth story" after the float, and "the dollar price of oil soon followed them down, falling below $12 a barrel by the end of 1998" [S3]. What was known July 2: the peg was gone and had followed weeks of visible strain; not known: how far the crisis or the oil-price decline would run.

## Class

Proposed class: `demand_shock`. Codebook clause: "A macro/health/policy event that abruptly changes demand." This is the only defensible fit — it is not a chokepoint disruption, OPEC decision, sanction, conflict escalation, or infrastructure attack; it is a currency/macro-policy event whose relevance to oil runs entirely through the demand channel.

Oil mechanism (codebook inclusion criterion 3), stated from retrieved evidence — and stated honestly as weaker than a supply-side event's mechanism: the baht float triggered a regional financial crisis that "developed doubts about the Asian growth story" [S3], and the newly industrialized Asian economies' growth had been "a factor boosting oil prices in the mid-1990s" on expectations of continued demand growth from a region whose *current* consumption share was, in Hamilton's own words, "modest" [S3] — so the mechanism runs through *expected future* demand growth being revised down, not a large immediate cut to current consumption. This is weaker and more indirect than a supply disruption's mechanism, and this dossier records that plainly rather than overstating it.
